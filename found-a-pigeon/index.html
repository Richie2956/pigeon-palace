Found a pigeon?

Calm, quick steps for the first hour. Warmth first, then fluids, then food.

A vet the same day for any of these

  • Any cat or dog contact, even without a visible wound
  • Open mouth breathing or gasping
  • Bleeding that does not stop with pressure in two to three minutes
  • Fits, tremor, head tilt or circling
  • Collapse, or a bird that cannot stand or hold its head up
  • A fracture, or a wing dragging on the floor
  • Blood in the droppings, or vomiting

Out of hours counts. No human painkillers, no aspirin and no antibiotics from the cupboard.

Does it need rescuing

A fledgling on the ground

A fully feathered fledgling on the ground that looks bright, with no shine on its neck yet, is usually fine. Its parents feed it only four to six times a day and visit roughly hourly.

Leave it, or move it a short distance out of traffic or away from cats, and come back in an hour. Assume it is orphaned only after three to four hours with no parent. Birds have no sense of smell, so handling it does not cause rejection.

A nestling

A nestling is part feathered, with yellow down on its head and a short tail. It cannot survive alone. Return it to the nest if you can reach it. If you cannot, it needs a rescuer or a vet the same day. Its parents will not find it if you move it to a bush.

A bird that lets you walk up to it

A pigeon that lets you walk up to it is almost always sick or injured. A bird that seems very tame and calm is a warning sign.

Rescue it if

  • it cannot fly
  • a wing droops
  • it is fluffed up and weak
  • it has visible wounds or blood
  • it wobbles, circles or holds its head at an angle
  • it has been breathing fast for two hours
  • it is tangled in string or netting
  • it has fly eggs or maggots on it
  • a cat or dog has had it
  • it has been hit by a car
  • it is oiled

When not to pick it up

Do not pick up a wild bird of any species that has a swollen head, closed or watering eyes, a twisted neck and breathing trouble. Those are among the avian influenza signs the RSPCA lists, and its advice is not to rescue or transport such a bird yourself. Ring the RSPCA on 0300 123 8960.

In a pigeon, a twisted neck on its own is far more likely to be paramyxovirus.

Do not touch dead wild birds. Report them on gov.uk or on 03459 33 55 77 in England, Scotland and Wales.

Catching and containing

Herd a weak bird into a corner or against a wall, drop a towel over it, and lift it inside the towel with its wings folded against its body. A box propped on a stick with food beneath it catches a bird that can still fly.

Wear gloves or use the towel, and wash your hands afterwards.

Put the bird in a cardboard box or cat carrier lined with a towel, with air holes, a lid that closes, and enough height that it cannot hit its head.

Keep the box indoors, in a quiet room with the door shut and children and pets out. Do not keep peeking. Never leave a caged bird outdoors, because predators reach through bars. In the car, belt the box onto a seat and keep the radio off.

The first hour

Warmth, then fluids, then food. A cold bird cannot absorb fluids, and a dehydrated bird cannot digest food.

  1. Warmth

    Put a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel at one end of the box, so the bird can move away from it, or a heat pad on low under half the box. A wet, cold or shocked bird needs this. A bright alert adult just needs a warm room.

    Panting or wings held out mean the bird is too hot. Hot water bottles do not last the night.

  2. Dark and quiet

    Leave the bird in the dark and quiet for an hour. Shock in a warm dark box should settle within about three hours.

  3. Fluids

    Only once the bird is warm and able to stand. Offer a shallow dish of oral rehydration solution. The emergency version is a pinch each of salt and sugar in a cup of water.

    Dip the tip of the beak into it for 20 to 30 seconds. Pigeons drink by suction and will usually take it. Never force the head down and never squirt liquid into the beak. Give no fluids by mouth to a bird that cannot hold its head up.

  4. Check it over

    Look at wounds, wings, legs, feet, mouth and droppings, and weigh the bird if you can. If it is more than mildly dehydrated, fluids by mouth will not be enough and it needs a vet.

  5. Food

    Last, and only once the bird has passed droppings. Thaw frozen peas and warm them in water, never in a microwave. With the bird wrapped in a towel, push one pea at a time into the back of the beak. Give five or six, then wait an hour for droppings before the next five or six.

    A bird that can feed itself gets seed and a dish of water in the box.

What not to do

  • Do not squirt liquid into the beak. Birds breathe it in easily and drown.
  • No milk, bread, porridge, biscuits or human baby food.
  • Do not feed a cold or dehydrated bird.
  • No human painkillers, and no aspirin.
  • No antibiotics from a cupboard.
  • Do not release the bird the moment it looks better. A netted or exhausted bird may be dehydrated and shocked, and a cat caught bird may be septic by evening. The exception is a window stunned bird with no injury that is flying normally after one or two hours.
  • Do not pull fishing line from a mouth, because there may be a hook. Cut netting only if it is stopping the bird breathing, leave about 10 cm attached, and take the bird in.
  • Do not pull a bleeding feather. Put cornflour on the shaft, press, and see a vet if it has not stopped in two to three minutes.

Cat or dog contact

A vet the same day. Every bird a cat has had in its mouth needs antibiotics the same day, whether or not you can find a wound.

Cat saliva carries Pasteurella, and septicaemia kills within hours. Treat any cat or dog contact as a vet visit today, even without a visible wound.

Who to ring

The RSPCA asks finders to take a small casualty to the nearest vet themselves where they safely can, and to ring only when they cannot.

    0300 123 8960

Ringed racing pigeons

A ringed racing pigeon that has sat in your garden for two days needs containing and reporting.

For a bird with a GB ring, text the ring number and the bird's condition to 07888 860186, or ring the RPRA on 01452 713529.
